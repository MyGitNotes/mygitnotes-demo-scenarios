# Research

Two days, no budget for anything fancy: a walk down the high street, four short chats with people who buy loose tea, and a look at what the other shops do. The point is to find the gap, not to copy the neighbours.

## What the other shops do

I photographed the windows and shelves of six tea sellers within a twenty-minute walk and sorted them by what the packaging promises. I have left their names out of the table on purpose, since the pattern matters, not the individual shop.

| Type | What it looks like | What it says | Gap for us |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heritage | Black tins, gold serif, crests | "Old and expensive" | Cold, formal, no one's kitchen |
| Wellness | Pastel pouches, botanical line art | "Good for you" | Interchangeable, says nothing about the tea |
| Origin | Plain kraft bags, a stamp and a rough map | "Honest, a bit austere" | Honest but hard to love |
| Gift | Boxed sets, ribbon | "Present" | Nobody keeps the box |

The only direction nobody owns is **warm and domestic**: something that looks as if it belongs on the shelf by the kettle.

## Four conversations

I asked everyone the same three questions: where do you keep your tea, what made you pick the last one, what would make you go back.

- Tea lives next to the kettle, in whatever tin or jar is nearest. Two people had kept a tin for years after the tea was gone.
- The last purchase was picked because the shop owner "knew what she was talking about", not because of the package.
- People go back when they can **find the same tea again without asking**. That is a naming and labelling job as much as a design one.

## What I am taking from it

1. The tin is the product. It is on the counter for years, so it has to be a tin you would keep.
2. The shop sign has to work from across the street, which rules out anything thin.
3. The identity should be built from a **handmade, slightly uneven** quality (glaze, stamp, paper) rather than from clean geometry alone. This is the thread for the [moodboard](moodboard.md).
